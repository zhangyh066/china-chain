# Seeing an industrial chain clearly

**Most people look at companies sideways** — a row of tickers, a column of price moves, a pile of ratios, all disconnected.

We wanted to look **vertically**: put a company back inside its chain, and first answer three questions.

- Who stands above it? (Two upstream tiers: direct suppliers, and the suppliers of those suppliers.)
- Who rests on it? (Two downstream tiers: direct customers, and their customers.)
- What holds it in place? (Where it sits on the chain: which links it belongs to, and who shares them.)

## Why this is worth doing

Because neither risk nor information **travels in isolation**.

When a separator-maker rallies, the cause may be the lithium price from two months earlier. When an automaker cuts output, the pressure lands first on the companies supplying its brakes and thermal management, then travels up into chips and copper.

**Look at one company alone and you see results. Follow the chain and you have a chance of seeing causes.**

## What this atlas covers

- **4,071** A-share listed companies (screened down from 5,918 — those with a real position on a chain), across **24** SW level-1 sectors
- **199** chain links and **18,404** company-to-link memberships
- **26,625** upstream/downstream edges covering 4,057 companies; **151** of them also carry a hand-curated value-chain map
- Quotes and financials are real public data, as of **2026-09-22**

## The data caveat, stated plainly

This is a demonstration of **how an industrial chain can be organised into a chart**, not a data product.
So what is real and what is inferred is written out, layer by layer:

- Company names, tickers, SW level-1 sectors, chain-link membership — **real, public**
- Quotes and financials (price, change, market cap, trailing P/E, gross and net margin, revenue growth) — **real public data** (East Money · akshare, as of 2026-09-22)
- Company profiles (business, key products, business scope, revenue breakdown, listing and registration facts) — **real public data** (THS · CNINFO · East Money, covering all 4,071 companies)
- Upstream/downstream edges — **inferred from real industry structure, illustrative and unverified**. Sharing a chain link is not a supplier relationship.

**None of it is an investment basis.**

## Three things we actually wanted to test

### 1. Structure carries more information than a list

A list of four thousand companies is just a column of tickers. Put each one back on the links it belongs to, and it becomes a structure you can read — who shares a chain with whom. **Position first, comparison second**: without position, comparison is only ever a size ranking.

### 2. A chain's value is in its tiers, not its length

Drawing only direct suppliers and direct customers gives you a flat picture of who deals with whom: it acknowledges the links but says nothing about transmission. Split the chain into tiers and a company's real neighbourhood becomes five to ten times its direct ties — **and it is precisely in those extra tiers that risk and demand actually travel**.

### 3. Events travel down the chain — in order

The same news means different things at different positions on the chain: when lithium prices rise, it is good news for the miners, a cost for the separator makers, and a quote revision two months away for the carmakers. **Transmission has direction, and it has lag** — the further downstream, the later the news arrives, and the shorter the window to react.

So this graph is really two maps: **read it upstream, it is a map of risk** — where the concentration is, what is replaceable, who gets hurt first; **read it downstream, it is a map of demand** — where volume expands, who benefits first.

Watching companies one by one, you can only wait for the outcome. Lay the chain out, and you can judge the order before the transmission arrives.

*A closing thought: China's manufacturing strength is not in single points but in the completeness of its chains. This map exists to make that completeness visible.*
